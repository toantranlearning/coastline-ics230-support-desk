What you are doing, and why

The portal came with a design document, DESIGN.md. In this lab you read it as a set of claims, test each claim against your own portal and its code, and record a verdict with evidence. Then you draw the portal's data-flow diagram, apply STRIDE, rank the threats, write the security requirements the document is missing, and apply one stopgap.

It practices lecture sections 2 to 10: design flaws, decomposition, STRIDE, security requirements, risk ranking, and the difference between a mitigation and a remediation. Plan about two and a half hours, in two sittings if you like.

Here is the ticket the team lead filed, and the lead's note on top of it.

Two other reports came in that may or may not be related. Support says a rep pulled up a customer who is not on their list. Someone in ops says they opened one of the site's files in a browser without signing in first. Neither has been looked at.

Team lead's note. The ticket says what people saw. It does not say which claims in the document are wrong, or what the mechanism is behind either report. Naming the mechanism is the review.

Part 1 - Audit the design document

  1. Sign in and walk the portal. Sign in as rmarsh.
    • Open every page the navigation offers: Customers, one customer's account, its call notes, Profile, Sign out, then sign back in.
    • You are learning what the portal does before you decide what it should refuse to do.
  2. Read DESIGN.md once, start to finish, without opening any code.
    • Read it the way a reviewer joining the team would. Do not verify anything yet.
    • Note the header: written in sprint 9, updated in sprint 19.
  3. Extract the claims. Go back through the document and pull out every sentence that asserts something about security: what is protected, who can reach what, what is checked, what is not served.
    • Quote the document's own words. A paraphrase is already an interpretation, and the wording is what you test.
    • Put each one in the claim register. The ten rows below are required. Add rows for anything else you find, including the Known limitations section.

Evidence is a file and line number, a command and its response, or a screenshot. "It looks fine" is not evidence.

  1. Test the deployment claim C10 and the authorization claim C5 with two requests. In the Cloud Shell terminal, which holds no portal session, request /data/seed.sql and then /data/portal.sqlite with curl -i, as the Requests and Developer Tools page shows.
    • Record the status line and the first few lines of each body. If you prefer the browser, sign out first, then open each address in your Web Preview tab.
    • You will see HTTP/1.1 200 OK and the file itself, both times. These are the two requests the rest of the lab refers to.
    • Write both verdicts with that evidence. Then open index.php line 15 and router.php lines 44 to 47, and say in one sentence why a check in every page did not protect these files (lecture section 7).
  2. Test the rep-scope claim C6. Count the customers the Customers page shows you as rmarsh.
    • Open data/seed.sql and read the assigned_rep column of the customers rows, lines 53 to 61. Count the rows assigned to rmarsh.
    • Open index.php line 18, the query that builds the list. Write one sentence on what the query does not ask.
    • Record three numbers: customers in the table, customers assigned to rmarsh, customers shown on the page.
  3. Test the data-protection claim C8. Open data/seed.sql lines 38 and 39: the tax_id column and the developer's comment beside it.
    • Find every page that writes tax_id into a response. Two do: customer.php line 65 and admin.php line 92. For each, say who can reach that page.
    • Add what the two requests in step 4 handed over. Then answer the claim in its own words: is the field exposed beyond the pages that require it?
  4. Test the input-handling claim C9, one half at a time. The two halves do not earn the same verdict.
    • First half, "checked before use": look for any code that inspects an incoming value and decides whether to accept it, such as a type, length, or format check. Start at customer.php lines 21 and 22 and notes.php lines 24 and 25. Record what you find, including if that is nothing.
    • Second half, "written so that the browser displays it as text": compare profile.php line 27 with layout.php line 42. Both write the role into the page. Module 01 lecture section 5 explains what the first does that the second does not.
    • Watch the difference. In your browser's developer tools, change the role cookie from rep to a value with markup in it, such as rep<em>!</em>, as the Requests and Developer Tools page shows. Reload Profile and capture the page.
    • You will see the same value rendered two ways on one page: as text in the profile card, as markup in the header bar. Record a verdict for each half.
  5. Test the authentication claims C4 and C3.
    • C4 says the role is "carried alongside the session". Open auth.php and find where the role is set, line 52, and where it is read back, line 113. Say where the role actually travels.
    • Change the role cookie's value to admin and reload. Record what changes in the navigation and which page you can now open. Name the principle from lecture section 8 the design breaks, and the weakness from lecture section 5 with its CWE.
    • C3 says the reset "confirms identity with the security question". Open reset-password.php lines 29 to 46 and the security_question and security_answer columns in data/seed.sql, lines 17 to 30. Compare a stored answer with the Home office shown on that rep's Profile page.
    • Record what the reset proves about the person requesting it, and your verdict.
  6. Settle C2 as written. The claim is "Passwords are stored hashed, never in the clear."
    • Open the users table in data/seed.sql, lines 9 to 30, and auth.php line 40. Record the column name and whether any password is stored as typed.
    • Give the verdict the words earn. Judging the strength of the hash is out of scope for this module; record only what the file shows.
  7. Finish the register. Give C1 and C7 their verdicts, then add rows.
    • C1: scripts/start.sh line 100 starts the server. Record the address it listens on, and whether anything in the project enforces "internal network only".
    • C7: notes.php line 31 and the created_by_name column in data/seed.sql, line 49. Say what is recorded about the author and what is not.
    • Add Unstated rows for what the document never decides: what happens on a failed sign-in, what is logged, who may see a tax identifier, how a session ends. Unstated is a finding too: it means nobody decided.
    • You should have at least ten rows, at least four False, at least one Verified, and at least two Unstated. If nothing came out false, you tested the document against itself rather than against the portal.

Part 2 - Model the threats

  1. Draw the data-flow diagram. Lecture section 4 draws one feature; the portal is small enough to draw whole at that level of detail.
    • Elements: the rep's browser, a visitor who has not signed in, the web server running router.php, the PHP pages, the three shared includes, and the SQLite file.
    • Draw every data flow between them and label what each carries, including the session cookie and the role cookie.
    • Draw a dashed line at each trust boundary: browser to server, server to the filesystem (the data directory is on the far side of this one), PHP to the database.
    • List the assets. For each, say which of confidentiality, integrity, and availability matters most, and why.
  2. Apply STRIDE. Ask the six questions of every element and every arrow, as lecture section 5 does for one arrow.
    • Write at least eight threats. Tie each one to a specific element or flow, not to the portal in general, and name its STRIDE category.
    • Add any threat that fits no letter. Business-logic abuse usually needs plain reasoning.
  3. Develop three threats in full. Choose three by likelihood and impact, and say why those three. For each, record:
    • the abuse case: the attacker's goal, what they need first, the steps, and the asset affected (lecture section 6);
    • the security requirement, written with "must", that two engineers could not disagree about (lecture section 6);
    • likelihood and impact with your reasoning, the priority, and the treatment: mitigate, eliminate, transfer, or accept (lecture section 9);
    • the control, the residual risk in one sentence, an owner with a date, and a verification test that names two outcomes and that someone else could run (lecture sections 6 and 9).

Troubleshooting

The two data requests redirect to the sign-in page

You requested a .php path, or your browser tab is still signed in. Check the address: the files are under /data/. In the browser, sign out first or use a private window.

The portal shows an error after sign-in

Open the Source Control panel and choose Discard All Changes, or run the baseline reset the Working in Google Cloud Shell page shows. Then sign in again.

The stopgap does not take effect

Confirm you edited router.php at the project root, that the new rule comes after the line that sets $path, and that you saved the file. Send the requests again from the terminal so you are not seeing a cached page.

The role cookie will not change

Edit it in the developer tools Application panel, then reload with the panel still open. If it reverts, you edited the session cookie by mistake; the one you want is named role.

Open notes and open book are both fine. Respond in your own words; do not copy from other sources.

Expected output

Your captures and your wording will differ; the findings must not. The lab passes when all of the following hold:

  • The claim register has at least ten rows, each with a verdict and evidence that is a command and response, a file and line, or a capture.
  • At least four claims are False, and the two data requests are shown returning 200 to an unauthenticated request.
  • The rep-scope claim is settled by three numbers: in the table, assigned to rmarsh, shown on the page.
  • The input-handling claim is split, one verdict per half, with the two renderings of the same value captured.
  • The password claim carries the verdict its words earn, with the column name as evidence.
  • The data-flow diagram shows every element, every labeled flow, and every trust boundary as a dashed line.
  • The threat register has at least eight threats, three developed in full with an abuse case, a security requirement, a rating with reasoning, a treatment, a control, the residual risk, an owner, and a two-outcome verification test.
  • The backlog merges the register and the threat model, every rating is yours with a reason, and exactly one finding is priority 1.
  • The stopgap is applied, both requests return 403, the portal still works, and the finding is marked mitigated with two reasons it is not a remediation.

Every other finding in the backlog is still open at the end of this lab, including the exposed data files themselves. That is expected; repairing them is out of scope for this module.

A documented blocker, with the exact error text, still earns full credit for its row.
