One real incident whose root cause is this module's topic. Say what went wrong and how it could have been stopped, then engage a classmate. The deep analysis belongs in your incident report.

The incident

In 2024 a financially motivated group that Mandiant tracks as UNC5537 signed in to the Snowflake cloud data accounts of many customer organizations, copied their data, and demanded payment. No software flaw was exploited and there is no CVE for the campaign. Every sign-in used a valid username and password. The passwords came from infostealer malware that had run on employee and contractor computers outside Snowflake, in some cases years earlier, and had been sold or traded since. At each affected account the same two things were true: the password was the only factor, and it had never been changed after it was exposed. One stolen password was the whole attack, account after account.

The facts, from the primary report and two further sources:

  • Mandiant and Snowflake notified about 165 organizations that may have been exposed. Mandiant published its report on June 10, 2024.
  • Mandiant found no evidence that the access came from a breach of Snowflake's own systems; every case it investigated traced to a stolen customer credential. Snowflake, CrowdStrike, and Mandiant said the same in a joint statement that described a targeted campaign against users with single-factor authentication.
  • The credentials came from infostealer malware families including Vidar, RedLine, Raccoon, Lumma, RisePro, and MetaStealer. The earliest infection tied to a credential used in the campaign dated to November 2020. About 80 percent of the accounts used had been exposed before.
  • The affected accounts had no multi-factor authentication, and the stolen passwords had not been changed since they were exposed, some for as long as four years.
  • Ticketmaster confirmed that its Snowflake environment was among those reached.

Sources: Mandiant's report on UNC5537 (opens in a new tab) (the primary report), TechTarget on the joint Snowflake, CrowdStrike, and Mandiant statement (opens in a new tab), the CISA alert of June 3, 2024 (opens in a new tab), and The Hacker News report (opens in a new tab).

Nobody guessed through a sign-in form and nobody broke a hash. The attacker presented a valid credential, and the sign-in did what it was built to do: it treated possession of the password as proof of identity. That is the gap between what a sign-in proves and what people assume it proves.

Initial post

Write 150 to 250 words answering both questions about the Snowflake campaign, in this module's vocabulary:

  1. What did they do wrong? Trace the failure through the sign-in as a chain of stages. Name the single factor that stood as the whole proof of identity, and which category it belongs to. Explain how a password taken from another machine's infection became a working credential here, using the lecture's name for that attack. Say what the owners did not do after the exposure, in the lecture's words on when a password is changed. Be precise about why "the password was correct" is not the same claim as "this is the account holder."
  2. How could it have been prevented? Name one specific control at one specific stage, and say why it stops this exact campaign rather than attacks in general. A second, independent factor at sign-in; a forced change once a credential is known to be exposed; and a check of each new password against a breach list are all fair game. Say which weakness in the lecture's naming your control closes.

A strong post names the mechanism, not only the headline.

Peer reply

Reply directly to at least one classmate, in at least 75 words. Open with their name and sign yours. Stress-test their prevention: would their control have stopped a valid, years-old credential presented by someone with no second factor, or only the attacks they thought of? Then add one control at a different stage of the chain, so the two of you have sketched defense in depth. Post early enough in the module for a real exchange.

Expectations

Full sentences, proper grammar and punctuation, no text-message shorthand. "I agree" is not a reply.
