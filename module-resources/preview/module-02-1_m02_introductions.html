M01 - Introductions Forum: Meet the Class

This is the first thing you do in the course, and it is one of the things that mark you as participating in the first week. Post your introduction and reply to at least one classmate. Students who do not participate in the first week are dropped, so please do not skip this.

It is also how I get to know where each of you is starting from. I read every introduction closely, because it tells me who has written a lot of code and who is newer, who has seen security before and who has not, and where I should slow down or add support. I keep what you tell me in mind all term: when a topic gives you trouble later, I already know your background, so I can point you to the right tutoring, resources, or a one-on-one before you fall behind rather than after. The more honest your picture, the better that works. There is no wrong answer here, and "I have never done this" is genuinely useful for me to know.

Initial post

Write a short introduction, about 150 to 250 words. Cover whatever you like, and at least these, honestly:

  1. Who you are: name, what you are studying, and where you are in your program.
  2. Your background: which languages you have used and how much, whether you have studied or worked with security before, and how comfortable you are with reading and changing PHP specifically, since this course leans on it. You work in Google Cloud Shell in your browser: you edit code in its editor and run a few short commands the course gives you. There is no version-control tooling to learn. Being newer is common and expected; the course advisories are a starting point, not a bar you must clear.
  3. What you are good at: the parts of programming or problem-solving that come easily to you. I use this to pair people up and to know who might help a classmate who is stuck.
  4. What you are not good at, or not yet: the parts you find hard, or that make you nervous about this course. This is the most useful thing you can tell me. It is not a confession; it tells me where to slow down and add support, for you and for the others who will quietly feel the same way.
  5. Your interests: what draws you to this, what you want out of secure coding, or where you hope it takes you.
  6. Your setup: the computer you will use (a home laptop, a school computer, or a Chromebook) and whether you already have a personal Google account. The lab runs in Google Cloud Shell in your browser, so this tells me who may need help getting it open in the first week.
  7. Something human: a project, a job, a hobby, anything that makes you more than a username.

Be candid on points 2 through 4 especially. I read these to decide where to add scaffolding, never to judge, and a student who overstates their comfort only makes it harder for me to help when a lab gets difficult. Telling me what you are not good at early is how it gets easier later.

Add a profile image while you are here (Account → Settings). A discussion of faces reads very differently from a discussion of gray silhouettes.

Peer reply

Reply to at least one classmate, about 75 to 100 words. Find someone you have something in common with, or someone whose goal interests you, and say so specifically. "Welcome!" is not a reply; "we both came to this from web dev and are both new to PHP; want to compare notes on the first lab?" is.

If you can, reply to someone who does not have a reply yet. Nobody should be left standing alone at the start of the term.

The discussion netiquette on the Communication, Interactions & Course Guidelines page applies here and every module after: be concise, be specific, disagree with ideas rather than people, and read a post aloud before you submit it. Here the stakes are low and friendly, which makes this the right place to build the habit.

Expectations

Full sentences, proper grammar and punctuation, no text-message shorthand. "I agree" is not a reply.

AI use on this forum. The syllabus policy governs; these are its terms for a discussion post.

✓Permitted

  • A grammar or spelling checker on your own writing.
  • Asking an AI to explain a term or an idea in general terms.

✕Not permitted

  • Having an AI write, draft, or rewrite your post or your reply.

If you used an AI to understand something, say so in one line at the end of your post: what you asked and what you used. You must be able to explain anything you post, on request.
