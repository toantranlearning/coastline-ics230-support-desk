What you are doing, and why

The midterm has two parts. One is the timed practical, taken in Canvas under the conditions the instructor states. This is the other: a written, take-home analysis of one documented real-world breach. You read what happened to another organization, name the root cause in this course's vocabulary, name the control that would have stopped it, and judge the response the way lecture section 13 judges one.

This is a written assignment, not a fix in your own environment. Plan about two hours. Choose a breach from the list below, or clear another with me first.

Scenario

You are writing the short analysis a security team circulates after a public breach: here is what happened, here is the root cause in our vocabulary, here is what would have prevented it, and here is how the response measured up. Your readers are other engineers who know the concepts but have not read the incident.

What you are given

Choose one documented breach whose root cause is a class this course has covered so far. Pick one with a public post-incident write-up or vendor advisory you can cite.

Instructions

  1. Summarize the incident in about 150 words, in your own words.
    • What was exposed, to whom, and how it was found.
    • Facts only, each one from a source you cite.
  2. Name the root cause in this course's vocabulary. Not "they got hacked."
    • Which class of weakness, with its CWE, at which point in the code or the design.
    • Where the source describes the flow, trace it: the value the attacker controlled, the trust boundary it crossed, and the decision or the sink that failed.
  3. Name the control that would have prevented it. This step carries the outcome.
    • The specific control, at the specific point, in the words the lecture that taught it uses.
    • Why that control stops this exact failure, not attacks in general.
  4. Judge the response on the three measures from lecture section 13, in two or three sentences each.
    • Detection. How was it found, and how long had the attacker been inside? Say what a breach found by an outsider tells you.
    • Containment speed. Once known, how fast was the access cut off?
    • Disclosure. Who was told, what were they told, and when? Where a notification law such as California's applied, say whether the timing met it.
  5. List your sources at the end, with at least one that is not a news article: the vendor advisory, a post-incident report, or a library database article.

Open notes and open book are both fine. Respond in your own words; do not copy from other sources.

AI use on this assignment. The syllabus policy governs; these are its terms for a written analysis.

✓Permitted, with disclosure

  • Asking an AI to explain a concept, an OWASP guideline, or a term in a breach report in general terms.
  • A grammar or spelling checker on your own writing.
  • AI help with the writing, if the AI DISCLOSURE line in your report states what you asked and what you used.

✕Not permitted

  • Submitting AI-generated analysis or writing without disclosing it.
  • Pasting this assignment into an AI tool and submitting what comes back, with or without edits.

You must be able to explain every sentence you submit, on request. Undisclosed AI use is handled as academic dishonesty under the syllabus.

Expected output

A complete analysis summarizes the incident from cited sources, names the root cause with its CWE and the point where it sat, names the control that would have prevented it and why it stops this exact failure, judges detection, containment speed, and disclosure, and lists the sources. The core is step 3: the control named precisely, not a general call for better security.

What to submit

Submit your analysis as a Markdown (.md) file, written as the Writing in Markdown page in the Course Orientation module shows, with your sources listed at the end.

At the top of the report, include this block, completed:

NAME: First Last

root cause was, and what control would have prevented it.
