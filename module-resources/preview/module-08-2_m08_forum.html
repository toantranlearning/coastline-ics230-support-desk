In mid-July 2025, attackers began mass-exploiting a chain of on-premises Microsoft SharePoint Server flaws dubbed "ToolShell." The core bug, CVE-2025-53770, rated 9.8 in severity, is an unauthenticated deserialization flaw enabling remote code execution: attackers sent crafted POST requests to the /_layouts/15/ToolPane.aspx endpoint, deployed ASPX webshells, and stole each server's MachineKey so they could forge authenticated requests and keep access even after the server was patched. Microsoft attributed the activity to three Chinese state-sponsored groups, active since at least July 7. The root cause was not exotic: internet-exposed, unpatched SharePoint servers. An earlier July patch had been incomplete and was bypassed, and organizations that had not reduced their exposure were found by attackers scanning the internet before they found themselves.

What happened next is the part that belongs to this module. Eye Security detected the campaign on July 18, then scanned more than 23,000 internet-facing SharePoint environments and confirmed roughly 400 compromised systems across some 145 organizations in 41 countries, including U.S. federal agencies. Read the report on the SharePoint mass exploitation (opens in a new tab) and Eye Security's account of its internet-wide scan (opens in a new tab) before posting. Notice that both sides of this incident ran the same kind of scan over the same internet: attackers scanning to find the compromisable, researchers scanning to count the compromised. This module asks what a scan result actually proves, and here the difference between the two scans was not the technique. It was who ran it first, and what the target's owners had never run at all.

Initial post

Write 150 to 250 words answering both questions, in this module's vocabulary:

  1. What did they do wrong? Trace the failure in scanning-and-coverage terms: what was in the victims' external attack surface, what their own testing coverage evidently never included, and why an exposed, unpatched endpoint is exactly what an internet-wide scan finds. Be precise about what the earlier incomplete patch meant for anyone treating "we patched in July" as evidence: an unvalidated claim of safety is an alert, not a finding.
  2. How could it have been prevented? Name at least one specific control at a specific point: for example, continuous external attack-surface scanning of the perimeter (defenders running the same scan Eye Security ran, before the attackers did), a rapid patch SLA for internet-facing systems, or removing on-premises SharePoint from direct internet exposure, and say why that control stops this exact attack rather than attacks in general.

Then add one or two sentences on the next module: access control. Next module a rep at TechCorp reaches admin pages and other reps' accounts just by editing a cookie, because the server trusts the browser to say who is an admin. What is wrong with letting the client carry its own permission?

A strong post commits to the mechanism. "They should have patched faster" is where the answer starts, not where it ends.

Peer reply

Read the other posts and reply directly to at least one classmate, in at least 75 words. Open with their name and sign yours at the bottom. Stress-test their prevention: would their control have found or stopped this exact exposure, or only the weaknesses they thought to look for? Then add one control at a different point in the chain, so the two of you have sketched defense in depth. Post early enough in the week for a real exchange.
