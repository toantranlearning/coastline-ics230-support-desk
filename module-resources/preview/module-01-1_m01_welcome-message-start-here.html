Welcome to ICS C230, Secure Coding and Design

You are here to learn how to build software that holds up when someone attacks it. That is a different skill from making software work.

This is a fully online, 16-module course. There are no required campus meetings. Everything happens online: lectures, labs, discussions, the midterm, and the design project.

What makes this course different

Most security courses teach you to find problems. This one teaches you to fix them, and then to prove the fix works.

Every vulnerability module runs the same loop:

  1. Break it. Exploit a real vulnerability by hand in the TechCorp portal, a deliberately insecure web application that runs as your own copy in Google Cloud Shell.
  2. Trace it. Follow the value from where it enters to the line where it became a command, and write the incident report. Your incident report is the deliverable; you repair the portal in your environment and keep the result.
  3. Understand it. See why the code allowed it, using the lecture's pattern.
  4. Build the fix. Write the secure version in your own code.
  5. Prove it. Re-run the attack against your fixed version. If it still succeeds, you are not done.
  6. Justify it. Name the principle your fix implements and explain why it works, in the report.

How the course is built

Three sources, all free:

  • OWASP Top 10:2025: the industry list of what actually goes wrong, plus the Cheat Sheets you will use as your fix reference.
  • CLARK modules: the source material behind the lecture pages. Free, peer-reviewed university modules from the National Cybersecurity Curriculum Repository.
  • The TechCorp portal: a deliberately insecure PHP application that runs as your own copy in Google Cloud Shell. You launch it from the Launch Course Environment page and change its code in the editor. There is nothing to install, and no account to set up. You attack it, study the provided scans, and repair it.

Your total cost is $0. There is no textbook to buy.

Start here, in this order

Click Next at the bottom of each page and work straight through this module. The pages are short, and the next one gives you the full week-one checklist with deadlines.

Two things are worth doing today rather than later:

  1. Check your computer against the requirements on the Course Materials, Software & Technology Requirements page. The bar is low. Any computer with a web browser and a keyboard will do, including a Chromebook or a school computer, but if anything blocks you, we need time to sort that out.
  2. Complete the Student Learning Contract. It is short, and it is part of how you are recorded as participating in week one. Students who do not participate in the first week are dropped.

One rule, before anything else

The hands-on work in this course means attacking real software, and you do that only in your own course environment. You never point it at another student's instance, the college's systems, or a website you are merely curious about. Testing anything that is not your own environment is both a violation of the student conduct code and, in most cases, a crime. Your environment runs in Google Cloud Shell, whose terms of service prohibit network scanning, so you do not run a scanner there either. In Module 07 you read a scan the instructor ran and judge its findings.

The full rule is on the Course Materials, Software & Technology Requirements page. Read it before you start the hands-on work.

About your instructor

I am Toan Tran, MS, CISSP, and I will be your facilitator this term. I have worked in technology for over 30 years as a software developer, database administrator, IT manager, and lead information architect, and I have taught at Coastline College for the past 17 years, where my focus is now cybersecurity.

My own path was not a straight line: a Computer Programming certificate from Cypress College during the dot-com boom, industry work, then a bachelor's in software engineering from Colorado Technical University, a master's in information assurance from Regis University, and the CISSP during the pandemic. If you are coming to this field sideways or later than you expected, you are in good company.

I stay current at conferences like DEF CON, Black Hat, and CISOA, and I still find this material genuinely fun and my hobbies are technology, security, and game development. I am glad you are here, and I am happy to connect on LinkedIn after the course ends to hear how your career is going.
