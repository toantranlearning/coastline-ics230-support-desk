This forum is optional, like the rest of the appendix. It covers one real incident whose root cause is a race condition. Say what went wrong and how it could have been stopped, then engage a classmate.

The incident

In May 2015, Egor Homakov, a security consultant at Sakurity, published a race condition in the gift card balance transfer on starbucks.com. Moving money from one card to another took two requests: the first saved the transfer details to the session, and the second confirmed it. He bought three cards with $5 on each. Then he sent the confirming request from two browser sessions on the same account at the same moment. On the sixth try, both confirmations went through. One $5 balance moved twice, and his three cards held $20 against the $15 he had paid. He spent the balance in a store to show it was real, then loaded $10 from his own credit card to pay Starbucks back. Nobody bypassed a login or injected anything. Two requests read the same balance before either one wrote it.

The facts, from Homakov's write-up and two reports:

  • The transfer checked a card's balance in one step and moved the money in a later one. Two confirmations that landed in that gap both passed the check. This is Homakov's reading of the behavior; Starbucks did not describe the code.
  • The race fired only sometimes. Homakov reports five failed attempts before the sixth landed.
  • Homakov emailed Starbucks' security address on March 23, 2015, and received a reply on April 29. He says the flaw was fixed about ten days after that. The dates are his account; Starbucks did not confirm them.
  • Starbucks told BBC News it "put safeguards in place to prevent replication" and did not say what they were. The statement is quoted in Schneier's post and Sullivan's report.
  • There is no CVE record for this case. The flaw was in one company's own website, not in software other people install.
  • Homakov's own recommended fix was a row lock taken before the read, with FOR UPDATE. That is his recommendation, not a description of what Starbucks changed.

Sources: Homakov's write-up of the Starbucks race condition (opens in a new tab), Bruce Schneier's post on the exploit (opens in a new tab), and Bob Sullivan's report (opens in a new tab).

The resource was a gift card balance, and the two requests came from two browser windows instead of a script. The shape is the one in lecture section 2: a read, a decision, and a write, with a window between them.

Initial post

Write 150 to 250 words answering both questions about the Starbucks incident, in this appendix's vocabulary:

  1. What did they do wrong? Trace the failure as a race: name the shared state the two requests touched, the check each one made, the act that followed, and the race window between them. Say why code that reads correctly one line at a time was still wrong the moment two confirmations ran side by side, and name the value that was lost.
  2. How could it have been prevented? Name at least one specific control at a specific point in that flow, and say why it stops this exact attack rather than attacks in general. A conditional UPDATE that carries the balance guard in its WHERE, a write lock taken before the read, and a unique constraint on the transfer record are all fair game.

A strong post commits to the mechanism. "They should have checked the balance more carefully" is where the answer starts, not where it ends.

Peer reply

Reply directly to at least one classmate, in at least 75 words. Open with their name and sign yours. Stress-test their prevention: would their control hold when the two confirmations run on two different server processes, or only in the case they pictured? Would a transaction boundary alone have closed the window? Then add one control at a different point in the flow, so the two of you have sketched defense in depth. Post early enough in the module for a real exchange.

Expectations

Full sentences, proper grammar and punctuation, no text-message shorthand. "I agree" is not a reply.
